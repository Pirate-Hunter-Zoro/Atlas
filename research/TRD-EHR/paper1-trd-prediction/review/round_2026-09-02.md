## The religion sensitivity, closed

The last analysis this round asked for. His supplied supplementary methods wanted
a sensitivity analysis comparing retention of religion against its removal, and
the question is really about the *missingness*: religion is unrecorded for 29.4%
of the cohort, differentially by age (43.8% at 18–29 against 17.5% at 65 or
older), and both representations make that absence readable, so a model can use
the field's silence as an age proxy without using the field. Removing is not
permuting, which is why the ablation slate does not answer it.

**Closed 2026-09-03 as a null on all six contrasts.** The deciding pair — the
religion-free narratives against the parity round's re-render-with-no-content-change
— are +0.0013 (95% CI −0.0017 to +0.0039) for logistic regression and −0.0031
(−0.0083 to +0.0024) for XGBoost; the feature arm is +0.0001 and +0.0002 with both
intervals inside ±0.0015 and both models on the published hyperparameters. By the
rule fixed on 2026-09-03 before the deciding numbers existed, the field is not
load-bearing, the write-up is held in reserve as `../reserve/religion_sensitivity.md` with
its numbers in `../reserve/religion_results/`, and the packet gained two clauses and nothing
else: Supplement M6 no longer says no such analysis was performed, and TRIPOD item
11 carries it beside the parity vitals re-run in the same "available from the
corresponding author" form.

### The prose verdict, and what it costs

The complaint is that the writing is dense: correct, but machine-sounding, and a
sentence has to be read three times to give up its meaning. He disputed no number
and no claim.

It is measurable. Over the body text (abstract through conclusions, tables and
figure captions excluded):

| | Manuscript | Readable target |
| --- | ---: | ---: |
| Sentences | 311 | — |
| Mean length | 26.2 words | 18-20 |
| Median | 24 | 17-18 |
| Over 35 words | 23% | under 10% |
| Longest | 86 words | — |
| Semicolons | 74 | a dozen |
| Em-dashes | 34 | a handful |

The punctuation counts are the diagnosis. Each semicolon and each em-dash aside
is a second or third claim welded into a sentence that already carried one, so
the reader has to hold a subject in memory across twenty words before the verb
resolves. Four habits produce it, and all four are mechanical:

1. **Claim, qualification and interpretation in one sentence.** Results,
   *Model discrimination*: "A paired bootstrap directly comparing these
   best-in-class models — the appropriate test, since both representations score
   the same held-out patients — yielded a non-significant difference of +0.008
   AUC (95% CI -0.003 to +0.019; Figure 3B). That interval is roughly a third the
   width of the marginal ones, so it reflects a genuine absence of advantage
   rather than insufficient power: the data exclude any embedded gain larger than
   about 0.02 AUC." Two sentences, five claims. The same content, unpacked:
   "Both representations score the same held-out patients, so we compared the two
   best models with a paired bootstrap. The difference was +0.008 AUC (95% CI
   -0.003 to +0.019; Figure 3B). This interval is about a third as wide as the
   separate intervals in Figure 3A. The comparison is therefore precise enough to
   rule out any embedding gain above roughly 0.02 AUC — an absence of advantage,
   not a shortage of data." Four sentences, no number moved, nothing hedged away.
2. **Sentences that describe the argument instead of making it.** "This
   highlights a fundamental methodological vulnerability: unless strict temporal
   separation is enforced ..." states that a point is about to be made. Make it.
3. **Abstraction where a picture would serve.** "Dense latent coordinates
   distribute information in a form naturally accommodated by a regularized
   linear combination, whereas one-coordinate-at-a-time tree splits no longer
   operate on individually meaningful clinical variables" is three concrete
   sentences wearing a coat.
4. **Stacked hedges.** One null defended three ways in a single sentence. State
   the null once, then state its bound once.

**The collision to raise with him.** Comment 84 cut Methods from 6,057 words to
1,851, and compression is what produces density. Shorter and less dense are not
the same instruction. The resolution is fewer claims with more room each, which
costs lines: four short sentences occupy more page than one long one. That is
what the Supplement M1-M13 structure is for — the main text carries the claim and
the M-section carries the machinery.

**The procedure, for when his sections land.** Numbers are frozen before any
plain-language pass, and no such pass re-derives a value. Simplification runs
one subsection at a time against an explicit budget (word count, median sentence
length, semicolon allowance) rather than an adjective. During that pass: no
em-dash aside inside a main clause, at most one subordinate clause per sentence,
no sentence whose only job is to introduce the next one, one hedge per claim in
its own sentence, verbs rather than nominalizations. Acceptance test is reading
it aloud. The last pass is the first author's own hand, for rhythm — three
sentences broken differently than a machine would break them is the cheapest
thing that stops prose sounding generated.

### The three word changes, with their exclusions

| From | To | Occurrences |
| --- | --- | ---: |
| deterministic | algorithmic | 18 — manuscript 8, supplement 7, checklist 2, cover letter 1 |
| patient | participant | ~261 — manuscript 101, supplement 136, checklist 18, cover letter 6 |
| trained classifier | machine learning model | 4 — manuscript 3, supplement 1 |

**Deterministic.** Not a swap. It follows comment [35], whose stated reason —
"you had the GPT generate it" — is wrong on the facts: fixed rules write the
narrative, no generative model participates, and that is what the word carried.
It is also load-bearing in the `manuscript.md` header rule, where it is the
sanctioned word for that claim and the reason "rule-based" was banned. Taking
"algorithmic" means rewriting that rule in the same edit and keeping the fact in
a clause — written by fixed rules, no generative model participating — or the
next editor restores the old word as a naming violation. Supplement M5's heading
carries it too.

**Patient.** Mechanical, and trap-laden. Four exclusions: the **title** carries
"Patient Narratives" and propagates to the cover letter and the checklist, so
changing it is an authors' decision rather than a sweep; **Supplement S6**
reproduces narratives verbatim and already holds the precedent for not
re-labelling the renderer's own output (`MDD-to-anchor gap`); quoted wording from
prior studies in *Comparison with prior work* stays as published; and figure
captions must not diverge from text baked into the PNGs. On that last one, the
plotting scripts and the cohort notebook were checked — "patients" appears in
comments and docstrings, and the axis labels in use are generic (Days, Density,
Frequency) — so no figure is expected to need redrawing, but that gets re-checked
against the built `.docx` before the change, not after.

**Trained classifier.** Cheap, and it destroys a distinction in at least two of
the four uses, where the phrase exists to contrast the fitted models with the
neighbor-weighted predictor, which fits nothing. "Machine learning model" covers
both arms and the contrast dissolves. Take the term where it is generic; where
the contrast is the point, say "the fitted models" against "the neighbor-weighted
predictor".

### The Discussion's take-home, which needs confirming

"Embedders are useful to match the standards other people have done." The reading
that fits his own *Comparison with prior work* section and the seven references he
added: the point worth leading with is that a general-purpose embedder reaches the
discrimination band the literature already reports — Lage 0.652, Lee 0.684, Walsh
0.51-0.64 externally, against our 0.657 — without a purpose-built EHR foundation
model. That is a reframing of *Principal findings*, from "the two representations
tie" to "the cheap route reaches the field's standard", and it is a better story
than the one now in the section. It is also his Discussion, so it is his call:
ask before rewriting it.
