Both are right. $(1\,2\,3)(1\,3) = (2\,3)$ and $(2\,3)(1\,3\,2) = (1\,2)$ are the two intermediate lines that carried it, and both check out point by point. Your margin arithmetic $(1\,2)(2\,3) = (1\,2\,3)$ is right too.

Now look at what you produced:
$$h = (1\,2), \qquad ghg^{-1} = (2\,3), \qquad g^2hg^{-2} = (1\,3).$$
You started with a transposition and every conjugate came out a transposition. That is the thing worth taking from this page: **conjugation cannot change cycle type.** Conjugating by $g$ just relabels the symbols — it sends the cycle $(a\,b)$ to $(g(a)\,g(b))$ — so the shape survives and only the names move. Check it against your own lines: $g$ sends $1\mapsto2,\ 2\mapsto3$, and indeed $(1\,2)$ went to $(2\,3)$.

On the margin: you are right that it is not a subgroup, and it never will be. The identity is not a conjugate of $h$ — if $ghg^{-1} = e$ then $h = e$ — so a set of conjugates of a non-identity element cannot contain $e$. Not being a subgroup is the normal state of affairs here, not a symptom.

But $(1\,2)(2\,3) = (1\,2\,3)$ is exactly the computation that answers the question I held back. You have just shown that multiplying two of your conjugates produces $g$ itself.
