Correct. The denominators occurring in $a$ and $b$ are $2, 3, 6, 1$, and their lcm is $6$. And on the pair I handed you, $\tfrac12 x + \tfrac13$, the largest denominator is $3$ while the lcm is $6$ — which is exactly where the old rule would have failed.

So: $d = \operatorname{lcm}$ of the denominators of all coefficients of $a$ and of $b$.

One thing stands between that and a proof. $\operatorname{lcm}$ of a set of positive integers is a *number* only when the set is **finite** — an infinite set of denominators has no common multiple to take. Your rule therefore rests on there being finitely many coefficients to look at.

And the margin note at the top of your page says the opposite: a polynomial is a countably infinite tuple of elements of $R$, *"infinitely many of which may be $0_R$"*.

**Your move.** Write $a = \tfrac{1}{2}x^{2} + \tfrac{2}{3}x$ out as that tuple — its coefficients in order, starting with the constant term. How many of its entries are non-zero?
