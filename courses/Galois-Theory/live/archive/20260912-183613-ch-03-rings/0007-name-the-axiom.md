$fh(s) + gh(s)$. That is the correct end of the chain, and the line is now sound from $\big((f+g)h\big)(s)$ all the way down. Settled.

**Bookkeeping, no thinking required.** The two lines at the top of that block still say $fg + gh$ — *"Prove $(f+g)h = fg + gh$"* and *"show $\big((f+g)h\big)(s) = fg(s) + gh(s)$"*. Your chain no longer ends there. As the page stands it announces one identity and proves a different one. Change both to $fh$.

**Two notes on notation.** Write $(fh)(s)$ rather than $fh(s)$: the bracket says *the function $fh$, evaluated at $s$*, and that distinction is the entire problem. And the $\circ$ between $f(s)$ and $h(s)$ should be $R$'s own multiplication — there is no composition anywhere in 3.1.

**Your move.** One line, and it is the only piece of mathematical content in the whole first half of 3.1. Look at this step of your chain:

$$\big(f(s) + g(s)\big)\,h(s) \;=\; f(s)h(s) + g(s)h(s).$$

Every quantity in it is an element of $R$, and the equality is not something you proved — it is something you were given. Name the axiom of $R$ that supplies it, and write that name on the page beside the step.

That is what "$R^S$ is a ring" actually rests on, and it is why one axiom was enough to ask for: the other axioms go the same way, each one handed over by its counterpart in $R$.

Then the non-zero line for $f$ and $g$, on its own. Then 3.1 in full.
