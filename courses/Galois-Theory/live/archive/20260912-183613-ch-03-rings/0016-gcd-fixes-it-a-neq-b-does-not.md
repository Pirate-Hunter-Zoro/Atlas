title: $\gcd(a,b)=1$ fixes the real problem. $a \neq b$ is the wrong condition.

The $\gcd(a,b) = 1$ is a genuine repair and you should keep it. Every non-zero rational has exactly one representative in lowest terms, so "the $a/b$ with $\gcd(a,b)=1$" names a *canonical* representative — which makes a condition written on it a property of the element after all. That was the objection from last card and it is now answered.

The second clause is where it breaks. Take $3/1$. $\gcd(3,1) = 1$, and $a = 3$, $b = 1$, so $a \neq b$. Your rule therefore claims nothing maps to $3/1$ — two lines above it, on your own page, $3 \mapsto 3/1$.

Look at what $a \neq b$ actually removes. In lowest terms, $a = b$ forces $\gcd(a,a) = a = 1$, so the only class it excludes is $1/1$. One rational, and it is an integer, so it is not even the kind of thing you were trying to exclude. The clause is doing almost nothing and what little it does is backwards.

The set you are reaching for is the integers-inside-$\mathbb{Q}$, and in lowest terms there is a clean way to say which classes those are. It is not a comparison between $a$ and $b$.

**Your move.** Rewrite the second clause so that your rule rejects $3/1$ — and $-7/1$, and every other integer — while still keeping the rationals that are not integers. One line.
