First line, correct, and it is the one carrying the weight. $a^{-1} \in R$, so
$e = aa^{-1}$ has the shape *(something in $R$) times $a$* — which is precisely
what membership in $(a)$ means. That is the entire content of "$a$ is a unit",
extracted.

The break is the last line:

$$es = s \in R.$$

True, and useless. $s \in R$ was your hypothesis — $s$ was chosen from $R$.
That line is true whether or not $a$ is a unit, whether or not $e \in (a)$, in
every ring there is. Nothing you proved in line one was used to get it.

$(a) = R$ is an equality of two sets. $(a) \subseteq R$ is free. The half that
needs an argument is $R \subseteq (a)$, so the line has to end with

$$s \in (a),$$

and yours ends somewhere else.

Look at what you invoked and then never used: *by the definition of $(a)$,
$\forall r \in R$* — and no $r$ appears again anywhere on the page. That $r$ is
the argument.

**Your move.** Rewrite the last line so it ends in $(a)$ rather than in $R$.

You have $e \in (a)$. Unfold that: the definition of $(a)$ promises you a
specific element of $R$ — name it. Then get from $s = es$ to $s \in (a)$.
