That is properness. A prime ideal is proper by definition, so $J \neq R$, so
there is some $r \in R$ with $r \notin J$ — and then $rx$ has a coefficient

## Exercise 3.26, part 2

Suppose $J$ is a proper prime ideal in an **integral domain** $R$.

2. Show that $J[x]$ is **not** a maximal proper ideal in $R[x]$.

### Everything the statement uses

- $R$: a commutative ring with identity $1$. An **integral domain**: no zero divisors — $ab = 0$ forces $a = 0$ or $b = 0$.
- $R[x]$: polynomials in $x$ with coefficients in $R$. A polynomial $a$ is its coefficient list $a_{0}, a_{1}, a_{2}, \dots$, all but finitely many zero; $a_{i}$ is the coefficient of $x^{i}$; $a_{0}$ is the constant term.
- *Ideal* $I$ of $R[x]$: closed under addition, and **absorbing** — $c \in I$, $r \in R[x]$ $\Rightarrow$ $rc \in I$.
- *Proper*: $I \neq R[x]$; equivalently $1 \notin I$.
- *Maximal proper ideal* $M$: proper, and **no ideal sits strictly between $M$ and $R[x]$**. So to show $J[x]$ is *not* maximal, you produce one ideal $I$ with $J[x] \subsetneq I \subsetneq R[x]$ — strictly bigger than $J[x]$, and still not everything.
- $J[x]$: the polynomials **every one of whose coefficients lies in $J$**.
- *The ideal generated by a set*: the smallest ideal containing it — everything you are forced to include once you close up under addition and under multiplication by arbitrary polynomials.
- Hypothesis in force: $J$ is proper, so $1 \notin J$.

So part 2 is a construction. You need one ideal wedged strictly between, and
the way to build it is to take $J[x]$ and throw in one extra polynomial. Which
one you throw in decides everything, and that is the question.
