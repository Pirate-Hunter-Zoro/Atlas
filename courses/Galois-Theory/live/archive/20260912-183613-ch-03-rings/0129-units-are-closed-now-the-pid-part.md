> and if $p \mid n$, then $p \mid r \cdot n$

Correct, and there is nothing dumb about a reversed implication caught
on the next line. That is the whole of it: $p \mid n$ gives $n = pk$,
so $r \cdot n = p(rk)$ and the witness is $rk$. Every representation of
$q/n$ therefore has a denominator divisible by $p$, so $q/n \notin R$,
so $n/q$ has no inverse in $R$.

**Part two of 3.17 is closed:** $R^{\times} = \{ n/q \in R : p \nmid n \}$.
I am writing up parts one and two. One part left.

**Exercise 3.17, part three.** With $p$ a fixed prime and

$$R = \left\{ x \in \mathbb{Q} : x = r/s \text{ for some } r,s \in \mathbb{Z}, s \neq 0, p \nmid s \right\},$$

**show that $R$ is a principal ideal domain.**

**Recall.**

- *Integral domain:* a commutative ring with $1$ and no zero divisors.
  $R$ sits inside $\mathbb{Q}$, so this half is free.
- *Ideal $I$ of $R$:* a nonempty subset closed under addition with
  $rx \in I$ for every $r \in R$ and every $x \in I$.
- *Principal ideal:* $(c) = \{ rc : r \in R \}$, the ideal generated by
  the single element $c$.
- *Principal ideal domain:* an integral domain in which **every** ideal
  is of the form $(c)$ for some $c$.
- *Membership in $R$:* $x \in R$ iff at least one representation $r/s$
  of $x$ has $p \nmid s$.
- *Units of $R$ (yours, just now):* $n/q \in R$ is a unit iff $p \nmid n$.
- *$a \mid b$:* there is an integer $k$ with $b = ak$.
