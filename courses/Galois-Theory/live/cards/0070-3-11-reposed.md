Correct: $2 \cdot 3 = 6$, so two non-zero classes multiply to zero in
$\mathbb{Z}/(6)$, and $6$ is not prime. That is the ladder done.

---

**3.11 (Garling).** Show that an element $a$ of an integral domain $R$ is prime
if and only if $R/(a)$ is an integral domain.

**Your move.** Prove it, both directions. Take $a \neq 0$ throughout — Garling's
definition of prime asks for that, and the statement needs it: $R/(0) \cong R$
is an integral domain while $0$ is not prime.

The two rungs you just did are the two directions in miniature: $\mathbb{Z}/(3)$
was the forward one, $\mathbb{Z}/(6)$ the backward one.

---

What the statement uses:

- *Prime* (Garling, p.~362): $a \neq 0$, $a$ not a unit, and whenever
  $a \mid bc$ then $a \mid b$ or $a \mid c$.
- *Integral domain:* commutative, $1 \neq 0$, and no zero divisors — $uv = 0$
  forces $u = 0$ or $v = 0$.
- $(a) = \{ra : r \in R\}$.
- $R/(a)$: classes $b + (a)$, with $(b+(a)) + (c+(a)) = (b+c) + (a)$ and
  $(b+(a))(c+(a)) = bc + (a)$. Its zero is $0 + (a)$, its one is $1 + (a)$.
- $a \mid b$ means $b = ra$ for some $r \in R$.
- *Yours, from rung one:* $b + (a) = 0 + (a)$ if and only if $a \mid b$.

Both clauses of "integral domain" have to be earned for $R/(a)$, not just the
zero-divisor one.
