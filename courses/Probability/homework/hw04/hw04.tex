\begin{problem}{2}
Whether or not it rains today depends on the weather of the last three days. Show how this system can be analyzed as a Markov chain. How many states are needed?
\end{problem}
% ===== SOLUTION 2 =====
Write the weather day by day, for instance rain, sun, rain, sun, sun. Combine the last three days into a single state. Each day is rain or sun, so there are $2^3 = 8$ possible states.
% ===== END SOLUTION 2 =====

\begin{problem}{3}
In Exercise 2, suppose that if it has rained for the past three days, then it will rain today with probability 0.8; if it did not rain for any of the past three days, then it will rain today with probability 0.2; and in any other case the weather today will, with probability 0.6, be the same as the weather yesterday. Determine $P$ for this Markov chain.
\end{problem}
% ===== SOLUTION 3 =====
Label the eight states, oldest day first, and index them $0$ to $7$ in that order:

$$\begin{gathered} 0:(S,S,S),\quad 1:(S,S,R),\quad 2:(S,R,S),\quad 3:(S,R,R), \\ 4:(R,S,S),\quad 5:(R,S,R),\quad 6:(R,R,S),\quad 7:(R,R,R). \end{gathered}$$

Then

$$P = \begin{pmatrix}
.8 & .2 & 0 & 0 & 0 & 0 & 0 & 0 \\
0 & 0 & .4 & .6 & 0 & 0 & 0 & 0 \\
0 & 0 & 0 & 0 & .6 & .4 & 0 & 0 \\
0 & 0 & 0 & 0 & 0 & 0 & .4 & .6 \\
.6 & .4 & 0 & 0 & 0 & 0 & 0 & 0 \\
0 & 0 & .4 & .6 & 0 & 0 & 0 & 0 \\
0 & 0 & 0 & 0 & .6 & .4 & 0 & 0 \\
0 & 0 & 0 & 0 & 0 & 0 & .2 & .8
\end{pmatrix}.$$
% ===== END SOLUTION 3 =====

\begin{problem}{5}
A Markov chain $\{X_n,\ n\ge 0\}$ with states $0,1,2$ has transition probability matrix

$$P=\begin{pmatrix} \tfrac12 & \tfrac13 & \tfrac16 \\[2pt] 0 & \tfrac13 & \tfrac23 \\[2pt] \tfrac12 & 0 & \tfrac12 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad p=\left(\tfrac14,\ \tfrac14,\ \tfrac12\right),$$

where $p$ is the initial distribution, the third entry following from $P\{X_0=0\}=P\{X_0=1\}=\tfrac14$. Find $E[X_3]$.
\end{problem}
% ===== SOLUTION 5 =====
Every row of $P$ sums to $1$, so $P$ is a transition matrix and $p$ is a distribution. The distribution of $X_3$ is $pP^3$: a $1\times n$ vector times an $n\times n$ matrix three times, which gives a $1\times n$ vector again.

$$P^2 = \begin{pmatrix} \tfrac13 & \tfrac5{18} & \tfrac7{18} \\[2pt] \tfrac13 & \tfrac19 & \tfrac59 \\[2pt] \tfrac12 & \tfrac16 & \tfrac13 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad
P^3 = \begin{pmatrix} \tfrac{13}{36} & \tfrac{11}{54} & \tfrac{47}{108} \\[2pt] \tfrac49 & \tfrac4{27} & \tfrac{11}{27} \\[2pt] \tfrac5{12} & \tfrac29 & \tfrac{13}{36} \end{pmatrix}.$$

Then

$$pP^3 = \left(\tfrac{59}{144},\ \tfrac{43}{216},\ \tfrac{169}{432}\right),$$

whose entries sum to $1$, and for a state taking the value $j$ with probability $P\{X_3=j\}$,

$$E[X_3] = P\{X_3=1\} + 2P\{X_3=2\} = \tfrac{43}{216} + 2\cdot\tfrac{169}{432} = \tfrac{53}{54}.$$
% ===== END SOLUTION 5 =====

\begin{problem}{34}
A flea moves around the vertices of a triangle in the following manner: whenever it is at vertex $i$ it moves to its clockwise neighbor vertex with probability $p_i$ and to the counterclockwise neighbor with probability $q_i=1-p_i$, $i=1,2,3$.

(a) Find the proportion of time that the flea is at each of the vertices.

(b) How often does the flea make a counterclockwise move that is then followed by five consecutive clockwise moves?
\end{problem}
% ===== SOLUTION 34 =====
Let $\alpha$ be the stationary distribution, so $\alpha P=\alpha$ with
\[
P=\begin{pmatrix}0&p_0&1-p_0\\ 1-p_1&0&p_1\\ p_2&1-p_2&0\end{pmatrix}.
\]
Writing out $\alpha P=\alpha$ gives
\[
\alpha_1(1-p_1)+\alpha_2p_2=\alpha_0,\qquad
\alpha_0p_0+\alpha_2(1-p_2)=\alpha_1,\qquad
\alpha_0(1-p_0)+\alpha_1p_1=\alpha_2,
\]
and carrying the right-hand sides across turns this into the homogeneous system
\[
\begin{pmatrix}-1&1-p_1&p_2\\ p_0&-1&1-p_2\\ 1-p_0&p_1&-1\end{pmatrix}
\begin{pmatrix}\alpha_0\\ \alpha_1\\ \alpha_2\end{pmatrix}
=\begin{pmatrix}0\\ 0\\ 0\end{pmatrix}.
\]
Its reduced row echelon form, computed with Mathematica, is
\[
\begin{pmatrix}
1&0&-\dfrac{1-p_1+p_1p_2}{1-p_0+p_0p_1}\\[8pt]
0&1&-\dfrac{1-p_2+p_0p_2}{1-p_0+p_0p_1}\\[8pt]
0&0&0
\end{pmatrix}.
\]
This is the result of
\begin{verbatim}
P = {{0, p0, 1 - p0}, {1 - p1, 0, p1}, {p2, 1 - p2, 0}};
RowReduce[Transpose[P - IdentityMatrix[3]]]
\end{verbatim}
Writing $N_0=1-p_1+p_1p_2$, $N_1=1-p_2+p_0p_2$ and $D=1-p_0+p_0p_1$, the two nonzero rows are
\[
\alpha_0-\frac{N_0}{D}\alpha_2=0,\qquad \alpha_1-\frac{N_1}{D}\alpha_2=0,
\]
so $\alpha_2=\alpha_0D/N_0$ and $\alpha_1=\alpha_0N_1/N_0$. The normalising condition then reads
\[
\alpha_0\left(1+\frac{N_1}{N_0}+\frac{D}{N_0}\right)=1,
\]
that is $\alpha_0=N_0/(N_0+N_1+D)$. With $S=N_0+N_1+D=3-p_0-p_1-p_2+p_0p_1+p_1p_2+p_2p_0$,
\[
\alpha_0=\frac{1-p_1+p_1p_2}{S},\qquad
\alpha_1=\frac{1-p_2+p_0p_2}{S},\qquad
\alpha_2=\frac{1-p_0+p_0p_1}{S}.
\]
These are the proportions of time the flea spends at vertices $0$, $1$ and $2$.

For part (b), condition on the vertex that the counterclockwise move leaves. At vertex $i$ the move is counterclockwise with probability $q_i=1-p_i$, after which the flea sits at $i-1$. The five clockwise moves then run
\[
i-1\to i\to i+1\to i-1\to i\to i+1,
\]
with probabilities $p_{i-1},p_i,p_{i+1},p_{i-1},p_i$, indices modulo $3$. The walk repeats its pattern after three steps, so the product of these five probabilities is $p_{i-1}^2p_i^2p_{i+1}$. Summing over the starting vertex gives the long-run fraction of moves that open such a run:
\[
\sum_i\alpha_i(1-p_i)p_{i-1}^2p_i^2p_{i+1}
=\alpha_0(1-p_0)p_0^2p_2^2p_1+\alpha_1(1-p_1)p_1^2p_0^2p_2+\alpha_2(1-p_2)p_2^2p_1^2p_0.
\]
Substituting $\alpha_i=N_i/S$ and factoring $p_0p_1p_2$ out of each product leaves
\[
\frac{p_0p_1p_2}{S}\Bigl(N_0p_0p_2(1-p_0)+N_1p_1p_0(1-p_1)+Dp_2p_1(1-p_2)\Bigr),
\]
with $N_0$, $N_1$, $D$ and $S$ as in part (a).
% ===== END SOLUTION 34 =====

\begin{problem}{40}
A particle moves on 12 points situated on a circle. At each step it is equally likely to move one step in the clockwise or in the counterclockwise direction. Find the mean number of steps for the particle to return to its starting position.
\end{problem}
% ===== SOLUTION 40 =====
The uniform distribution on the 12 points is the stationary distribution, so $\pi_i = 1/12$ for every $i$. Proposition 4.4, in Section 4.4 of Chapter 4 (printed page 204), says that in an irreducible recurrent chain the long-run proportion of time in state $i$ is $\pi_i=1/m_i$, where $m_i$ is the expected number of transitions from $i$ back to $i$. So
$$m_i = \frac{1}{\pi_i} = 12.$$
% ===== END SOLUTION 40 =====

\begin{problem}{52}
A taxi driver provides service in two zones of a city. Fares picked up in zone A will have destinations in zone A with probability $0.6$ or in zone B with probability $0.4$. Fares picked up in zone B will have destinations in zone A with probability $0.3$ or in zone B with probability $0.7$. The driver's expected profit for a trip entirely in zone A is $6$; for a trip entirely in zone B is $8$; and for a trip that involves both zones is $12$. Find the taxi driver's average profit per trip.
\end{problem}
% ===== SOLUTION 52 =====
With states $A$ and $B$ the pickup zone, the trips give
\[
P=\begin{pmatrix}0.6&0.4\\0.3&0.7\end{pmatrix},
\]
and the four profits are
\[
AA\to 6,\qquad BB\to 8,\qquad AB\to 12,\qquad BA\to 12.
\]
So
\[
E[\text{profit}]=6\,p(AA)+8\,p(BB)+12\,p(AB)+12\,p(BA),
\]
and with $p(AA)=p_A(0.6)$, $p(BB)=p_B(0.7)$, $p(AB)=p_A(0.4)$ and $p(BA)=p_B(0.3)$,
\[
E[\text{profit}]=6\,p_A\cdot 0.6+8\,p_B\cdot 0.7+12\,p_A\cdot 0.4+12\,p_B\cdot 0.3.
\]
The stationary distribution solves $[\,p_A\ \ p_B\,]P=[\,p_A\ \ p_B\,]$, that is
\[
0.6p_A+0.3p_B=p_A,\qquad 0.4p_A+0.7p_B=p_B,
\]
whose solution is
\[
p_A=\frac{3}{7},\qquad p_B=\frac{4}{7}.
\]
Substituting,
\[
E[\text{profit}]=6\cdot\frac37\cdot\frac35+8\cdot\frac47\cdot\frac{7}{10}+12\cdot\frac37\cdot\frac25+12\cdot\frac47\cdot\frac{3}{10}
=\frac{54+112+72+72}{35}=\frac{310}{35}=\frac{62}{7}\approx 8.857.
\]
% ===== END SOLUTION 52 =====

\begin{problem}{60}
The following is the transition probability matrix of a Markov chain with states $1,2,3,4$:
\[
P=\begin{pmatrix}.4&.3&.2&.1\\ .2&.2&.2&.4\\ .25&.25&.5&0\\ .2&.1&.4&.3\end{pmatrix}.
\]
If $X_0=1$,
\begin{enumerate}
\item[(a)] find the probability that state 3 is entered before state 4;
\item[(b)] find the mean number of transitions until either state 3 or state 4 is entered.
\end{enumerate}
\end{problem}
% ===== SOLUTION 60 =====
For part (a), let $u_i=\Pr(3\text{ before }4\mid \text{start at }i)$, with states $1$--$4$. Then
\[
u_3=1,\qquad u_4=0.
\]
The first-step equations are
\[
u_2=0.2u_1+0.2u_2+0.2,\qquad u_1=0.4u_1+0.3u_2+0.2.
\]
Rearranging,
\[
0.2u_1-0.8u_2=-0.2,\qquad -0.6u_1+0.3u_2=-0.2,
\]
or, after scaling,
\[
u_1-4u_2=-1,\qquad -6u_1+3u_2=-2.
\]
The first gives $u_1=4u_2-1$, so the second becomes
\[
-6(4u_2-1)+3u_2=-2 \;\Longleftrightarrow\; -21u_2=-8,
\]
hence
\[
u_2=\frac{8}{21},\qquad u_1=\frac{32}{21}-\frac{21}{21}=\frac{11}{21}.
\]

For part (b), let $n_i$ be the expected number of transitions until state $3$ or state $4$ is entered, given that the chain starts at $i$, with states $1$--$4$. Then
\[
n_3=0,\qquad n_4=0.
\]
A transition out of state $1$ or state $2$ is taken with certainty, so one step is charged on every branch, plus the steps remaining after it:
\[
n_1=0.4(n_1+1)+0.3(n_2+1)+0.2\cdot1+0.1\cdot1,\qquad
n_2=0.2(n_1+1)+0.2(n_2+1)+0.2\cdot1+0.4\cdot1.
\]
Collecting the constants, using $0.4+0.3+0.2+0.1=1$ and $0.2+0.2+0.2+0.4=1$, gives
\[
n_1=0.4n_1+0.3n_2+1,\qquad n_2=0.2n_1+0.2n_2+1,
\]
that is,
\[
0.6n_1-0.3n_2=1,\qquad -0.2n_1+0.8n_2=1.
\]
Scaling both lines by $10$,
\[
6n_1-3n_2=10,\qquad -2n_1+8n_2=10.
\]
Three times the second line added to the first gives $21n_2=40$, hence
\[
n_2=\frac{40}{21},\qquad n_1=\frac{10+3\cdot\frac{40}{21}}{6}=\frac{55}{21}.
\]
Starting at state $1$, the mean number of transitions until state $3$ or state $4$ is entered is $\frac{55}{21}\approx2.619$. Starting at state $2$ it is $\frac{40}{21}\approx1.905$.
% ===== END SOLUTION 60 =====

\begin{problem}{76}
On a chessboard compute the expected number of plays it takes a knight, starting in one of the four corners of the chessboard, to return to its initial position if we assume that at each play it is equally likely to choose any of its legal moves. (No other pieces are on the board.)

Hint: make use of Example 4.36.
\end{problem}
% ===== SOLUTION 76 =====
Example 4.36 (printed page 241) considers a particle on a connected graph with unit arc weights and gives $\pi_i=d_i/\sum_j d_j$, where $d_i$ is the number of arcs leaving vertex $i$. On the knight graph the vertices are the $64$ squares and the arcs are the legal knight moves, so $d_i$ is the number of legal moves from square $i$.

The number of legal moves from each square, row by row on the board, is
\[
\begin{matrix}
2&3&4&4&4&4&3&2\\
3&4&6&6&6&6&4&3\\
4&6&8&8&8&8&6&4\\
4&6&8&8&8&8&6&4\\
4&6&8&8&8&8&6&4\\
4&6&8&8&8&8&6&4\\
3&4&6&6&6&6&4&3\\
2&3&4&4&4&4&3&2
\end{matrix}
\]
and these entries sum to $\sum_i d_i=336$.

For a corner square $d_{\text{corner}}=2$, hence
\[
\pi_{\text{corner}}=\frac{d_{\text{corner}}}{\sum_i d_i}=\frac{2}{336}=\frac{1}{168}.
\]

The mean return time is the reciprocal of this long-run proportion of time,
\[
\frac{1}{1/168}=168.
\]

So the expected number of plays from a corner back to that corner is $168$.

The two textbook statements used: Example 4.36 (printed page 241) gives $\pi_i=d_i/\sum_j d_j$ for the random walk on a connected graph, and Proposition 4.4, in Section 4.4 of Chapter 4 (printed page 204), gives $\pi_j=1/m_j$, where $m_j$ is the expected number of transitions from $j$ back to $j$.
% ===== END SOLUTION 76 =====
