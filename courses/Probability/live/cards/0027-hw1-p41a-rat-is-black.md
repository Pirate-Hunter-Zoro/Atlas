Line 2 is the hard step and it is right: the brown sibling is $bb$, so each
parent handed down a $b$; both parents are black, so each also carries a $B$;
both parents are $Bb$. You got there in one line and it is the whole reason the
problem is stated the way it is.

Now read your own conditioning bar back:

$$P(BB \mid bb \text{ sibling}, \text{ parents } Bb, Bb).$$

Every hypothesis in the problem is behind that bar except one. The rat is a
**black** rat — Ross says so in the first clause — and nothing in your
calculation used it. What you computed, $\frac12 \cdot \frac12 = \frac14$, is
the probability that an arbitrary offspring of $Bb \times Bb$ is $BB$, black or
brown, chosen before anyone looked at it.

Your four equally likely pairs are $BB$, $Bb$, $bB$, $bb$. One of them is
incompatible with what you were told and did not use.

---

Put that hypothesis behind the bar and compute again. It is the same operation
as the die on card 0017: throw away what the hypothesis rules out, then
renormalise over what survives.
